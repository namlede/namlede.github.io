Lichen

Lichens are composite organisms formed by a symbiosis between a fungus (the mycobiont) and one or more photosynthetic partners — either a green alga, a cyanobacterium, or both (the photobiont). The fungus provides the structural scaffold and microenvironment, while the photobiont fixes carbon via photosynthesis and shares the resulting sugars. For a long time the textbook picture was a clean two-player mutualism, but in 2016 Toby Spribille and collaborators found that many lichens also harbor basidiomycete yeasts embedded in the cortex, which may contribute to chemical defense — so the "simple" symbiosis is at minimum a three-way affair. Lichens reproduce in two broad modes: the fungal partner can reproduce sexually via spores (which then must re-acquire a photobiont from the environment), or the whole composite can propagate asexually through fragments like soredia or isidia that carry both partners together. Their growth rates are extremely slow — often millimeters per year — which makes them useful for dating rock surfaces (lichenometry). They're also famously pollution-sensitive because they absorb water and dissolved gases directly across their surface with no roots or cuticle to filter contaminants, so their presence or absence on trees and rocks is a rough proxy for local air quality, particularly SO₂ levels.
